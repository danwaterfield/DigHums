## 1. Citation Chains — the intellectual wiring

The most significant new finding is the **citation chain structure** across the philosophy, medical, conduct, and science texts. These show how ideas flow between the categories that the thesis argues are connected:

### The Locke chain
Locke (759 citations across the corpus) is cited by:
- **Condillac** *Origin of Knowledge* (60) — French reception, sensationist epistemology
- **Reid** *Inquiry into Human Mind* (39) — Scottish common-sense critique
- **Dugald Stewart** *Philosophy of Human Mind* (33) — Stewart mediates Locke to the 1790s
- **Hutcheson** *Beauty and Virtue* (20) — moral sense theory built on Lockean foundations
- **Edgeworth** *Practical Education* (embedded in "SomeThoughtsConcerningEducation" count)

This is the pipeline: Locke → Condillac/Reid/Hutcheson → Stewart → Edgeworth. The Lockean epistemology that dominates conduct literature (as the source-shift analysis showed) travels through specific intermediaries. Stewart is the key late-century node — he synthesises Locke, Reid, and Hartley for the 1790s educational writers.

### The medical chain: Sydenham → Cheyne/Cullen/Whytt
Sydenham (58 citations) is cited by Cullen (14), Whytt (11), Mandeville (8), and — crucially — by **Locke's *Some Thoughts Concerning Education*** (7). Locke and Sydenham were close friends; the fact that the conduct book cites the physician confirms the medical-conduct fusion at the very origin of the tradition.

### The Paine–Burke axis
Burke (261 citations) is cited overwhelmingly by **Paine's *Rights of Man*** (256 of 261). This is a single-text dominance issue, but it's also substantively important: the 1790s political debate is almost entirely a Burke-Paine dyad in our corpus. Malthus (89 citations of Godwin) is the other 1790s citation axis.

### The Hutcheson–Smith–Shaftesbury triangle
Hutcheson cites Shaftesbury (23 times in *Beauty and Virtue*). Smith cites Hutcheson (9 times in *Moral Sentiments*). This confirms the politeness-philosophy transmission: Shaftesbury → Hutcheson → Smith. All three sat in The Club's intellectual lineage.

### Condillac and the Edgeworths
Edgeworth's *Practical Education* cites Condillac 29 times (23 in Vol 1, 6 in Vol 2). This is the French sensationist epistemology entering British conduct literature directly. Edgeworth is the author who bridges Lockean British empiricism and Condillac's continental sensationism — both feeding into the conduct-book framework.

### Crichton's sources
Crichton's *Mental Derangement* (1798) — the text containing the earliest description of what we'd now call ADHD — cites **Reid** (12), **Hume** (15), and **Priestley** (9). He's building his clinical framework for attention on Scottish common-sense philosophy (Reid), associationist psychology (Hume/Hartley), and natural philosophy (Priestley). The attention-as-faculty model comes from philosophy, not from clinical observation alone.

## 4. Key Implications

### Joseph Johnson as the missing publisher node
The bookseller data has Cadell (Strand, establishment) and Dodsley (Pall Mall, literary) but is missing **Joseph Johnson** (St Paul's Churchyard, radical/dissenting). Johnson published Wollstonecraft's *Vindication*, Priestley's scientific works, Godwin's *Political Justice*, Paine's *Rights of Man*, Darwin's *Zoonomia*, Barbauld's poems, and Hays's *Appeal*. He's the publisher of the **counter-normative** texts — the ones that challenge the conduct-book framework. Adding him would complete the publishing triangle: Cadell (establishment), Dodsley (literary), Johnson (radical).

### Hackney and the Dissenting academies
Hackney appears 194 times, mostly in history and political texts. The Hackney New College (Dissenting academy, 1786–96) educated Priestley's students and was the institutional base of rational Dissent. This is the educational alternative to the Anglican via media — adding it to the venue data would let you map the geographical separation between Anglican norm-setting (Strand, Fleet Street, coffeehouses) and Dissenting critique (Hackney, Newington Green where Wollstonecraft ran her school).

### Bethlem/Bedlam as a venue
58 combined mentions across 33 texts. This is the institutional site of pathologisation — where the via media's behavioral norm gets enforced on those who fail to meet it. It's not yet in the venue database. Adding it (with Moorfields location pre-1815, Lambeth post-1815) would close the spatial loop: the conduct books prescribe behavior from the Strand; the coffeehouses perform it; Bethlem confines those who can't.

### The Inns of Court as intellectual spaces
The Temple complex (1,531 mentions) is massively over-represented — partly because it's a common word, but the legal profession is genuinely important. Blackstone's *Commentaries* (now in the corpus) codifies the legal framework within which lunacy, vagrancy, and behavioral regulation operate. The Inns of Court are where the normative subject becomes a legal subject.
