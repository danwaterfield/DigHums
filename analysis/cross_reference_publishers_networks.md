## 1. Cadell's Strand — the normative canon published from one address

Thomas Cadell I, operating from the Strand (successor to Andrew Millar), published:

| Author | Title | Year |
|--------|-------|------|
| Burke | *Sublime and Beautiful* | 1757 |
| Chapone | *Letters on the Improvement of the Mind* | 1773 |
| Gibbon | *Decline and Fall* (6 vols) | 1776–88 |
| Adam Smith | *Wealth of Nations* (2 vols) | 1776 |
| Frances Burney | *Evelina* | 1778 |
| Johnson | *Lives of the Poets* | 1781 |
| Frances Burney | *Cecilia* | 1782 |
| Charlotte Smith | *Emmeline* | 1788 |
| Radcliffe | *Udolpho* | 1794 |
| Frances Burney | *Camilla* | 1796 |
| Hannah More | *Strictures on Female Education* | 1799 |

The philosophical aesthetics, the conduct manual, the history, the political economy, the sentimental novel, the Gothic novel, and the evangelical conduct book — all from the same shop. The conduct literature, politeness philosophy, and fiction aren't separate discourses happening to overlap — they're being produced, marketed, and sold from the same Strand address to the same reading public.

## 2. Millar → Cadell: the Enlightenment publishing succession

| Publisher | Location | Active | Works | Key authors |
|-----------|----------|--------|------:|-------------|
| Andrew Millar | Buchanan's Head, Strand | 1730s–60s | 20 | Fielding, Hume, Thomson, Adam Smith (*Moral Sentiments*) |
| Thomas Cadell I | Strand (successor) | 1760s–1800s | 18 | Burke, Gibbon, Smith (*Wealth*), Burney, More, Radcliffe |

The intellectual project passes through two publishers on the same street across 50 years. Millar publishes the mid-century Enlightenment (Hume's philosophy, Fielding's novels, Smith's moral philosophy, Thomson's poetry). Cadell inherits the list and publishes the late-century applications (Burke's political thought, Gibbon's history, Smith's economics, Burney's conduct-novels, More's evangelical conduct). The "canon" isn't retrospectively assembled — it was commercially assembled in real time by two booksellers in direct succession.

## 3. Locke's publisher is Pope's publisher

Jacob Tonson I at Shakespeare's Head, Fleet Street published:
- Locke, *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1690)
- Addison & Steele, *Tatler* and *Spectator*

Jacob Tonson II at the same shop published:
- Pope, *Essay on Man* (1733)

The dominant conduct-literature authority (Locke) and the dominant literary authority (Pope) came off the same press. The Lockean epistemology that grounds the conduct books and the Augustan literary culture that provides their aesthetic register were commercially entangled from the start.

## 4. Dodsley's Pall Mall — the experimental wing

Robert Dodsley at Tully's Head, Pall Mall published Shaftesbury's *Characteristics*, Burke's *Sublime*, Johnson's *Rasselas*, Sterne's *Tristram Shandy*, and Goldsmith's *Vicar of Wakefield*. This is the wittier, more philosophically adventurous wing — Sterne and Goldsmith rather than Richardson and Burney.

Dodsley's sign ("Tully's Head" = Cicero) advertises classical authority. His list represents the writers who interrogate sensibility rather than prescribing it: Sterne's *Tristram Shandy* is the novel most directly theorising attention, distraction, and the nervous body — published by a bookseller whose sign invokes the classical rhetorical tradition.

## 9. 1765–67: peak density

14 key figures active simultaneously in 1765–67: Arthur Young, Burke, Chapone, Fordyce, Gibbon, Goldsmith, Gregory, More, Hume, Johnson, Priestley, Rousseau, Smith, Sterne.

This is the moment when:
- Arthur Young publishes his novels (1767–68)
- The Allen-Burney marriage connects the families (October 1767)
- Frances Burney is 15–16 and beginning to write
- The entire intellectual field that shapes her work is simultaneously alive, mostly in London, and connected through The Club, the Strand booksellers, and the Thrale salon

## 10. The Hume–Edinburgh axis

David Hume's early publications were all Edinburgh-based (Fleming & Alison, Kincaid, Donaldson) before his work migrated to London publishers (Millar for the *Enquiry Concerning Morals*, 1751). Charles Burney corresponded directly with Hume (2 letters, 1764–66). This Edinburgh–London axis matters: the Scottish Enlightenment (Hume, Smith, Reid, Robertson) provides the philosophical framework, but its dissemination — and its absorption into conduct literature — happens through London publishers and London sociability networks.

## 11. Implications for the thesis

The publisher and network data shows that the "construction of behavioral normality" wasn't an abstract discursive process — it had a **material infrastructure**:

1. **Two booksellers** (Millar, then Cadell) on the Strand commercially assembled the canon of politeness philosophy, conduct literature, and sentimental fiction over 50 years
2. **One coffeehouse** (Turk's Head) provided the physical space where the key authors met
3. **One family network** (Burney → Thrale → Johnson → Burke → Smith) transmitted ideas between the philosophical, literary, and conduct registers
4. **One domestic library** (Charles Burney's) put the relevant texts in the novelist's childhood environment

The via media behavioral norm wasn't theorised in isolation and then applied — it was produced by a specific social network operating through specific commercial and institutional channels in specific London locations. The gazetteer's venue data maps the spaces; the publisher data maps the commerce; the correspondence data maps the personal connections. Together they show that "the Anglican state's construction of normality" is less an abstract process than a concrete social-commercial operation centred on the Strand, Fleet Street, and the coffeehouses between them.
