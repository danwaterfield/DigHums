# Arthur Young vs Frances Burney — Stylometric & Thematic Comparison

**Date:** 2026-03-25
**Texts compared:**
- Arthur Young: *The Adventures of Miss Lucy Watson* (1768), *The History of Julia Benson* (1784)
- Frances Burney: *Evelina* (1778), *Cecilia* (1782), *Camilla* (1796), *The Wanderer* (1814)

**Source:** Perry, *Novel Relations* (footnotes on Young's fiction)

**Family connection:** Martha Allen married Arthur Young (1765); her sister Elizabeth Allen married Dr Charles Burney (October 1767). Young was Frances Burney's step-uncle. His novels were published 1767–68, when Frances was 15–16.

## 10. Interpretation

The Lucy Watson–Evelina convergence is the strongest signal:

- **Same form:** Epistolary, with female protagonist writing to confidantes and authority figures
- **Same plot engine:** Father-daughter authority over marriage; consent, obedience, and the heroine's navigation of suitors
- **Same emotional shape:** Falling sentiment arc with Q1 peak
- **Same vocal register:** High exclamation, high direct address, sentimental interjection
- **Near-identical metrics:** Emotion density (5.23 vs 5.49), exclamation rate (6.9 vs 7.0)

Where they diverge marks Burney's transformation of the mode:

- **Internalisation:** Burney halves the explicit courtship vocabulary (10.6 vs 23.0); the emotional conflict moves inward
- **Colloquial voice:** Burney uses contractions 6× more often — her characters sound like people talking
- **Topographic density:** Burney populates her London with specific sites (Ranelagh, Vauxhall, Pantheon) that become narrative environments, not just place-names
- **Honour over fortune:** Burney foregrounds "honour" where Young foregrounds "fortune" — the stakes become moral rather than economic

The proximity — familial, temporal, formal — is striking. Young published an epistolary sentimental novel about a young woman navigating paternal authority and the marriage market in 1768. His new step-niece Frances was 16. Ten years later she published one herself, transformed by psychological depth and social specificity.

Whether this represents direct influence or shared conventions of the sentimental novel is the interpretive question. The computational evidence establishes the formal and thematic parallels; the biographical evidence (family connection, contemporaneity, shared social world) makes direct exposure highly probable.

## 11. Caveats

- **OCR quality:** Both Young texts are 18th-century OCR with long-s artifacts. Julia Benson's long-s normalisation is incomplete (e.g., "foon" for "soon"), which deflates its vocabulary overlap scores.
- **Attribution:** Julia Benson is published anonymously; the Young attribution comes from DNB and Perry, not the title page.
- **Missing texts:** Young's other two novels — *The Adventures of Emmera* (1767) and *The History of Sir Charles Beaufort* (1768) — are behind the ECCO paywall. A complete comparison would require all four.
- **Corpus asymmetry:** Burney's corpus is much larger (~1.1M words vs ~120K for Young), which affects type-token ratios and vocabulary metrics.
